Benchmark Analysis

Your TTS Model Passes English Benchmarks.
It May Be Changing Facts in Arabic.

A VoiceArena analysis of 44,387 human evaluation votes across six languages reveals that multilingual voice AI fails along a specific and predictable fault line — and the consequences extend well beyond voice quality into factual accuracy.

VoiceArena Research · Josh Talks AI Data: Apr 30 – May 4, 2026 44,387 votes · 857 evaluators · 6 languages 9 models evaluated
Executive Summary

Across 44,387 pairwise evaluations scored by 857 native-language human evaluators in English (en-US), Hindi (hi-IN), Arabic (ar-MSA), Japanese (ja-JP), Portuguese Brazilian (pt-BR), and Vietnamese (vi-VN), three findings emerge that single-number leaderboards cannot show.

First: Model quality does not degrade uniformly across languages. The same model can rank second in one language and last in another. Rank inversions of 20–40 percentage points are common, not exceptional.

Second: The most consequential failure mode in multilingual TTS is not robotic voice, not irregular pacing, not mild mispronunciation. It is factual substitution — models producing a different number, year, amount, or code than what appears in the input text. Native evaluators across Arabic, Hindi, Vietnamese, and Portuguese flagged this at rates that constitute a reliability concern for production deployment.

Third: The failure concentrates along a specific fault line: structured numeric content in non-English languages. One model's hallucination rate on numeric sentences reaches 23% in Arabic while remaining 1.5% in English — on the same model, in the same evaluation window.

What This Evaluation Is and Is Not

VoiceArena uses pairwise preference evaluation. A human evaluator — a native speaker of the target language — is presented with two audio outputs generated by two different TTS models from the same input sentence. The evaluator listens to both, selects the preferred output (or declares a tie), and optionally writes a comment explaining their choice. A structured defect-tagging interface allows evaluators to mark specific failure types: mild mispronunciation, severe mispronunciation, unnatural/robotic voice, irregular pacing, hallucination or extra content, missing word, and noise/distortion.

This analysis covers evaluations conducted between April 30 and May 4, 2026. Nine TTS models were evaluated: Google DeepMind Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS, ElevenLabs Eleven v3, xAI Grok TTS, Cartesia Sonic-3, OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts, Microsoft Azure Dragon HD Omni, MiniMax Speech 2.8 HD, Sarvam AI Bulbul-V3 (Hindi only), and Fish Audio S2 Pro (English only). Input sentences spanned four content categories: Customer Support & Contact Center, Media & Entertainment, General Conversation & AI Assistants, and Content Creation & Education.

Cartesia Sonic-3: Records the lowest win rate of any model in English at 22.0% — ranking last in en-US. In Hindi and Portuguese Brazilian, it reaches 53.2% and 53.3% respectively, placing third in both languages. In Japanese, it records 12.6%, placing last and more than 30 points behind the next-lowest model. The nature of its Japanese failures is documented in evaluator comments and is examined in Section 5.

OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts: Its 57.0% win rate in Japanese is notably higher than its global average of 39.7% and its performance in Hindi (26.2%) or Vietnamese (23.6%). Its Japanese wins concentrate in STEM education, financial confirmation, and news content — sentence types with heavy alphanumeric content where its outputs were judged more accurately than competing models.

Key finding

For every model except Gemini and ElevenLabs, the difference between best-language and worst-language win rate exceeds 30 percentage points. A model chosen on the basis of aggregate or English-only benchmark performance may underperform substantially in a specific target language.

Section 3

What Human Evaluators Actually Penalize

Across the 23,753 votes with structured defect tags, six failure categories account for the overwhelming majority of flags: mild mispronunciation (4,727 mentions), severe mispronunciation (3,418), unnatural/robotic voice (3,189), irregular pacing (1,818), hallucination or extra content (1,035), and missing word (692).

These categories are not equal in how they affect evaluator preference. The data shows that evaluators frequently vote for a model they describe as "robotic" or "slightly mispronounced" when the alternative model commits a more disqualifying failure. Gemini receives 1,021 "unnatural_robotic_voice" tags across its appearances — and still wins 86.9% of contested votes. Evaluators are tolerating mechanical voice quality when the output is otherwise accurate and well-paced.

Section 4 — Core Finding

The Fault Line: Numbers, Codes, and Structured Content

The defect taxonomy above measures failure in degrees — robotic voice is worse than mild mispronunciation, severe mispronunciation is worse than robotic voice. But one defect category sits outside this quality gradient entirely, because it is not a voice quality failure at all. It is a factual accuracy failure. It is what happens when TTS models encounter numbers, alphanumeric codes, dates, and financial figures in non-English text — and produce different ones.

Native evaluators, who hear these outputs in the context of their own language and daily experience, began flagging a pattern early: not that the model mispronounced a number, but that it produced a different number. A refund of 244 dirhams became an unintelligible sequence. A train number was read as an entirely different sequence. A historical year was altered. An Avogadro constant was changed to a wrong value. A card activation number was hallucinated mid-sequence. These are not voice quality failures. They are factual accuracy failures.

In English, numerals make almost no difference to Grok's hallucination rate: 1.5% with numerals, 1.0% without. In Arabic, removing numerals drops the rate from 23.0% to 6.5% — a 3.5× reduction. This strongly suggests that the hallucination is triggered specifically by how numeric and alphanumeric content interacts with the Arabic text rendering and speech synthesis pipeline, not by a general Arabic language weakness. The evaluation data does not reveal the mechanism; that determination requires internal model analysis. What the data establishes is the behavioral pattern at output.

Section 5

Model-by-Model: What the Defect Signatures Reveal

The following characterizations are grounded in evaluator defect tags and written comments. No inference is made about model architecture or training.

Google DeepMind Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS

Gemini leads in every language and holds the lowest defect rate (20.9%) and lowest hallucination rate (2.69%) in the dataset. Its losses come primarily from ElevenLabs on naturalness. Evaluators choose Gemini even when they note it sounds robotic — because its alternatives fail on dimensions they penalize more heavily. Its failure mode, when it occurs, is voice quality. Factual accuracy is not its problem.

ElevenLabs Eleven v3

Consistent second place globally (71.1%) and across all six languages (60.2%–75.5%). ElevenLabs has the highest noise/distortion rate among top-tier models at 7.49% — evaluators use words like "echo," "static," and "artifact" — but continue to prefer it because the voice sounds more human than most alternatives. The trade-off is real and measurable: ElevenLabs has a technical audio problem, not a pronunciation problem.

xAI Grok TTS

Examined in depth in Section 4. Its English performance (61.9%) is competitive. Its non-English performance and its numeric hallucination rate are not. One pattern worth noting separately from the number errors: evaluators in Arabic repeatedly flagged the addition of laughter — audible laughing sounds — in outputs from sentences with no laughter in the input. This appeared in multiple independent evaluations of the same sentence types, across separate evaluators.

Arabic (ar-MSA) Warranty explanation xAI Grok TTS
"There is laughter at the beginning that is not present in the text."
Arabic (ar-MSA) E-commerce — delivery confirmation xAI Grok TTS
"Audio contains laughing, which doesn't appear in text."

This is a separate failure category from number hallucination — not a factual substitution but an affective generation error — and it appeared consistently enough in the Arabic data to be noted as a distinct pattern.

Cartesia Sonic-3

Cartesia has the sharpest English-to-non-English performance gap in the evaluation for a different reason than Grok. Its failures are not factual — they are acoustic. Evaluators describe pacing that is too fast, echo or reverb artifacts, and pauses at incorrect phrase boundaries. The irregular pacing tag rate (21.7% of appearances) is the highest of any model in the dataset. In English, where the comparison field is strongest, these artifacts cost Cartesia nearly every contested vote (22.0% win rate). In Hindi and Portuguese Brazilian, where competing models fail on different dimensions, these same artifacts are tolerated more often.

English (en-US) Customer support Cartesia Sonic-3
"Too fast paced, echo, rushed and monotone — pause was misplaced."
Portuguese BR (pt-BR) Customer support Cartesia Sonic-3
"The audio is too fast, which makes it harder to follow."

OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts

Records 57.0% in Japanese — its highest win rate in any language and 17 points above its global average of 39.7%. Its wins in Japanese concentrate in STEM, financial, and news content: sentence types with dense alphanumeric content where its outputs were judged more accurate than competing models. It records 26.2% in Hindi and 23.6% in Vietnamese. Its dominant defect across all languages is robotic/unnatural voice (31.8% of appearances). This evaluation covers gpt-4o-mini-tts specifically; the findings do not extend to other OpenAI voice offerings.

Microsoft Azure Dragon HD Omni

Records 12.6% in Japanese — the lowest single-language win rate of any model in this evaluation. The robotic voice tag accounts for 32.1% of its total appearances across all languages, the highest of any model. In Japanese specifically, evaluators documented misreadings of compound kanji words and proper nouns with a specificity that indicates systematic failure on this word class, not random pronunciation error.

Japanese (ja-JP) Transit and location content Microsoft Azure Dragon HD Omni
"Mispronounced 東京医科歯科大学病院, 濃霧, and お茶の水駅. The word JAL was also read incorrectly. Robotic throughout."
Japanese (ja-JP) Hospital public address Microsoft Azure Dragon HD Omni
"Incorrect reading, deleted text, and missing text."

These are not accent or naturalness complaints. They are precision failures on named entities and compound words that a native Japanese listener immediately identifies as wrong.

MiniMax Speech 2.8 HD

Evaluated in four languages (hi-IN, ar-MSA, pt-BR, vi-VN). Performs at the mid-tier median in Hindi and Arabic (49.8%, 44.7%), falls to 25.7% in Portuguese Brazilian. No single dominant failure type distinguishes its defect profile. Its hallucination rate (6.06%) places it in the same band as Cartesia and Azure Dragon.

Sarvam AI Bulbul-V3

Evaluated in Hindi only. At 53.1% — third place in Hindi — Sarvam outperforms the general-purpose mid-tier models in that language (OpenAI: 26.2%, Azure Dragon: 29.2%, xAI Grok: 36.7%) but does not close the gap to Gemini (82.0%) or ElevenLabs (73.3%). The data establishes where it places among models evaluated in Hindi; it was not evaluated in other languages.

Section 6

What This Means for Multilingual TTS Deployment

One finding in this dataset is specific enough to be actionable rather than advisory.

The numeric hallucination rate for xAI Grok TTS in Arabic Customer Support content reaches 14% overall and 23% on numeric sentences. The Customer Support sub-buckets in this evaluation include banking confirmations, utility complaints, flight rescheduling, and telecom alerts — sentence templates that are among the most commonly deployed TTS use cases globally. A team evaluating Grok for an Arabic-language IVR or voice assistant using English-only benchmarks, or aggregate leaderboard position, would see a model ranked competitively against mid-tier alternatives. They would not see the 23% figure. That figure is only visible in a native-language human evaluation on content that matches the deployment context.

The broader implication is not that English evaluations are useless. It is that they are incomplete. And the dimension where they are incomplete — structured numeric content in non-English languages — happens to be the dimension that matters most when TTS is deployed in banking, healthcare, government services, and transport.

Limitations

Limitations

01

Pairwise win rates are opponent-dependent. A model's win rate in a given language reflects the quality of models it was paired against, not only its absolute quality. Cartesia Sonic-3's higher win rate in Hindi compared to English may partly reflect a weaker mid-tier comparison field in Hindi, not an improvement in Cartesia's Hindi performance. Head-to-head pairwise comparisons between specific model pairs were not the primary analysis unit and are not reported here.

02

The evaluation covers a five-day window. Evaluations were conducted between April 30 and May 4, 2026. Model behavior may change with updates. The findings reflect these specific model versions during this specific period. Longitudinal consistency was not tested.

03

Numeric hallucination was not tested with controlled inputs. The distinction between numeric and non-numeric sentences in this analysis uses a simple classifier: any sentence containing at least one digit. A controlled evaluation using identical sentences with only numbers changed — and deploying the same sentence across all language variants — would provide stronger evidence for the specificity of the numeric failure pattern. The findings here are observational, not experimental.

04

Defect tagging reflects evaluator judgment. The defect taxonomy was applied by human evaluators, not by an automated classifier. Inter-evaluator consistency on the boundary between "mild mispronunciation" and "severe mispronunciation," or between "hallucination" and "missing word," was not formally measured. Written comments provide a partial check on tag reliability.

05

This analysis covers gpt-4o-mini-tts specifically. OpenAI offers multiple voice-capable products. The observations about OpenAI's performance in this document apply only to the gpt-4o-mini-tts model evaluated here and should not be generalized to other OpenAI voice offerings.

Methodology

Methodology

VoiceArena uses pairwise preference evaluation modeled after the approach introduced by Chatbot Arena for LLM evaluation. For TTS, the methodology is adapted as follows: a single input sentence is synthesized by two TTS models. A native speaker of the target language listens to both outputs and selects a preference (A wins, B wins, or tie). The evaluator may also provide a written comment and apply structured defect tags to either output.

Model pairings are determined by the evaluation pipeline. Evaluators are not informed which model produced which output. The same input sentence may appear in multiple evaluation pairs across the dataset.

Sentences span four content categories and dozens of sub-buckets within each, including banking confirmations, flight announcements, audiobook narration, news reading, general conversation, STEM education, government public address, and others. Approximately 69% of sentences contain numeric content.

Full methodology documentation is available at voicearena.com/tts-methodology. The live leaderboard and evaluation interface are at voicearena.com.

Conclusion

Conclusion

Three things are true simultaneously in this dataset, and holding all three at once is the point.

Gemini wins by a margin that is not close. At 86.9% globally and never below 80.9% in any language, it is the only model in this evaluation with a stable, cross-language quality lead. For teams choosing a general-purpose multilingual TTS model today, the leaderboard is not ambiguous.

Below that tier, the rankings are language-specific, unstable, and in some cases reversed. A model second in English may be last in Japanese. A model last in English may be third in Hindi. Aggregate leaderboard position conceals more than it reveals for teams deploying in a specific non-English language.

The finding that is not visible in any existing leaderboard is the numeric hallucination pattern. Across Customer Support content in Arabic, a hallucination rate of 23% on numeric sentences means that roughly one in four outputs containing a number — an account balance, a reference code, a room number, a flight number — will contain content that a native speaker identifies as different from the input. At that rate, this is not an edge case to be monitored. It is a baseline behavior to be measured before deployment.

The measurement instrument that surfaced this finding was not automated. It was 857 people listening to audio in their own language and writing down what they heard. That is the data. The leaderboard is at voicearena.com.
